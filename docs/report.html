1Summary

Five findings from every Formula 1 World Championship race from 1950 to the 2026 Brazilian Grand Prix.

  1. Dominance comes in waves. The results fall into six eras, and every one of them has come to an end. Ferrari is the only team with eras of dominance in very different periods of F1.
  2. Mercedes in 2014-2016 is the most dominant team spell. It won 86.3% of races on the 3-year average centred on 2015, the highest peak of any team, and took a record eight consecutive Constructors' Championships (2014-2021).
  3. But the highest peak of all belongs to an engine. Across the three views (teams, engines and factories), the top value is the Ford engine's 87.8% in 1973, just above Mercedes' 86.3% in 2015. Ford also spent 16 seasons at 40% or more, more than anyone in any of the three views.
  4. The badge on the car hides part of the story. One engine can win with several teams at once: Renault had two great eras with different partners, and the change at the top in 2025-2026 (McLaren, then Mercedes) is really continuity of the Mercedes engine. Grouping teams by factory joins Tyrrell and Mercedes, two eras about 40 years apart, and shows that the name "mercedes" mixed two different teams.
  5. What we see depends on how we look. The smoothing window and the 40% threshold are my decisions, and changing them changes the picture: McLaren's 93.75% in 1988 becomes 58.3% on a 3-year average, and a 50% threshold leaves out Cooper, which peaked at 49.7%.

2The question and the metric

The question behind this report is simple: which teams dominated each era of Formula 1? To answer it, we need a way to measure dominance that works the same in 1950 and in 2026.

The obvious candidate would be championship points, but points don't travel well through time. The points system and the number of races per season have changed many times, so points are not comparable across eras.

That's why the metric here is the percentage of races won each season. Winning is the clearest sign of dominance, and a percentage puts every season on the same scale. In 1950 Alfa Romeo won 6 of the 6 races counted: 100%. In 1988 McLaren won 15 of 16: 93.75%. A season of 6 races and one of 24, like 2024 or 2025, can be compared directly.

How to read the numbers

Single seasons are noisy, so most values in this report are a 3-year centred moving average: each season is averaged with the one before and the one after. This shows eras instead of year-to-year jumps, but it also flattens peaks. McLaren's 93.75% in 1988, for example, becomes 58.3%.

A team counts as dominant if its moving average reaches at least 40% in some season. Both choices are mine, and section 6 checks how much the conclusions change with others.

3Teams, era by era

Counting wins by official constructor name, ten teams reach the 40% line at some point. Ordered by when they peaked, they draw six eras.

1950-1960The first champions

F1 began in 1950, and Alfa Romeo dominated the first two seasons. In 1950 it won every race counted (6 of 6); in 1951, 4 of 7, with Ferrari taking the other 3. Its peak, 78.6% in 1950, sits at the very start of the timeline, where the moving average is least reliable.

In 1952-53, with Alberto Ascari, Ferrari emerged as the dominant team: 7 of 7 races in 1952 and 7 of 8 in 1953. Its 76.8% in 1952 is still Ferrari's highest peak.

After them, Mercedes showed its ability to win with Juan Manuel Fangio in 1954-55. At the end of the decade, Cooper won two titles (1959-60) with Jack Brabham thanks to its revolutionary rear-engined car, a design every team copied soon after. Cooper peaked at 49.7% in 1959.

A warning about names: the 1950s Mercedes and today's Mercedes share a name but are different teams. The current one returned to F1 in 2010 and became dominant from 2014. Section 5 separates them.

1960-1975Lotus, Brabham and Tyrrell

Lotus emerged as the dominant team with Jim Clark, peaking at 53.3% in 1964, while Ferrari suffered a major setback. In the mid-1960s, Brabham had its best years.

In the early 1970s Tyrrell dominated with Jackie Stewart, although it only crossed the 40% line in one season: 1972, at 43.4%. In the mid-1970s, Ferrari returned strongly with Niki Lauda.

1975-1985A decade without a leader

This was a fairly diverse era, with no single team standing out above the rest. Ferrari and Williams appear as the most successful teams, closely followed by McLaren, Lotus and, towards the end of the era, Renault.

1985-2000McLaren and Williams take turns

The diversity disappeared and two teams took turns at the top. McLaren ruled the late 1980s with Alain Prost and Ayrton Senna: 15 wins out of 16 races in 1988 and a peak of 64.6% in 1989. Williams followed in the early 1990s, peaking at 56.2% in 1992, with 7 seasons at 40% or more.

Benetton won its first titles with Michael Schumacher in 1994-95, crossing the line by a few tenths (40.3% in 1994). McLaren returned to the front in the late 1990s with Mika Häkkinen, and at the end of the era Ferrari regained its strength. Lotus and Brabham disappeared for good, never to return among the most successful teams.

2000-2010Ferrari and Schumacher

In the early 2000s Ferrari returned to the top as the most successful team, with Michael Schumacher. Williams began its definitive decline. Renault returned to the top, matching McLaren in wins, but faded again by the end of the decade. McLaren remained one of the leading teams, but Ferrari dominated the record books of this era.

Adding up all its eras, Ferrari spent 12 seasons at 40% or more, more than any other team.

2010-2026Red Bull and Mercedes

Red Bull improved quickly and dominated 2010-2013 with Sebastian Vettel. Then Mercedes began the most dominant period in F1 history, with Lewis Hamilton: 86.3% of races won on the 3-year average centred on 2015, and a record eight consecutive Constructors' Championships (2014-2021).

Red Bull caught up in the early 2020s with Max Verstappen, peaking at 74.2% in 2022. Ferrari and McLaren remained top teams, generally well behind both. McLaren improved and won the 2025 Constructors' Championship.

After a sharp decline, Mercedes is dominant again. In 2026 (provisional, 20 races so far) it has won 60% of the races, ahead of McLaren and Red Bull (15% each) and Ferrari (10%).

4Engines: the hidden story

A team builds its own car, but many teams bought their engines from someone else. So one engine could win with several teams at the same time, and engines tell a different story from teams.

Only Ferrari, which builds both, looks the same in both views: a peak of 76.8% in 1952 and 12 seasons at 40% or more, as a team and as an engine.

Ford: the longest and strongest era

The Ford Cosworth DFV was sold to almost any team (Lotus, Tyrrell, McLaren, Williams, Brabham…). Its wins were spread across several teams, so Ford never appears among the dominant teams. But as an engine it had one of the longest and strongest eras in F1 history, from the late 1960s to the early 1980s.

Its peak, 87.8% in 1973, is the highest value in the whole analysis, above Mercedes' 86.3% in 2015. And its 16 seasons at 40% or more are the most in any of the three views.

Renault: two eras, different teams

Renault had two great eras with different teams: in the 1990s with Williams and Benetton, when it peaked at 74.0% in 1996, and in 2010-2013 with Red Bull. In total, 10 seasons at 40% or more. As a team it was never as dominant: it reaches 30% at its best, but not 40%.

Other engines at the top

Climax, which powered Cooper and Lotus, peaked at 63.0% in 1960 and stayed at 40% or more for 6 seasons. TAG, McLaren's engine in the mid-1980s, reached 45.8% in 1985.

Honda shows how an engine and a team can read the same season differently. In 1988 both Honda and McLaren won 15 of 16 races (93.75%), but on the 3-year average Honda gets 75.0% and McLaren 58.3%, because in the neighbouring seasons Honda also won with other teams.

Continuity that looks like change

Among teams, McLaren led in 2025 and Mercedes in 2026. But both use Mercedes engines, so the Mercedes engine dominates both seasons. Winning with customer teams too, the Mercedes engine spent 15 seasons at 40% or more, against 10 for the Mercedes team.

One caveat: engines are counted by their official name. Honda's recent era with Red Bull is split between "honda" and "honda-rbpt" (Red Bull Powertrains, built by Honda), so it looks smaller than it really was: "honda-rbpt" alone peaks at 55.4% in 2024.

5Factories and lineages

Teams change names and owners. Counting by name treats Toro Rosso and AlphaTauri as two different teams, although they came from the same factory.

To group them, I used the F1DB constructors chronology, which lists the names each factory has had and the years of each one. Each factory is labelled with its first and last name: the one that started as Tyrrell (1970-1998) and is Mercedes today becomes tyrrell → mercedes, and Toro Rosso (2006-2019) belongs to minardi → racing-bulls. Teams that never changed name, like Ferrari, keep their own.

Wins are matched using both the name and the year. That matters: Renault, for example, appears in the Enstone family (toleman → alpine) in two separate periods, 2002-2011 and 2016-2020.

The eras barely change, but the stories do

The peaks of dominance are almost the same as when we count by name, because most name changes happened between eras of dominance, not during them. What changes is how some histories are told: Benetton now appears as part of the Enstone family ("toleman → alpine", 40.3% in 1994), and Red Bull includes its Stewart years ("stewart → red-bull", 74.2% in 2022).

One factory, two eras 40 years apart

"tyrrell → mercedes" shows one factory with two great eras about 40 years apart: Tyrrell with Jackie Stewart in the early 1970s and Mercedes with Lewis Hamilton in 2014-2020. In between sits Brawn's 2009 season, when the factory won 8 of 17 races (47.1%), ahead of "stewart → red-bull" with 6 (35.3%).

That's why its 9 seasons at 40% or more are not all Mercedes seasons: they also include Tyrrell's 1972 season.

Two teams called "mercedes"

Grouping by factory also fixes an error of the analysis by name: the name "mercedes" mixed two different teams, the 1950s works team and the current one. Using both the name and the year separates them. The 1950s team is now its own factory, "mercedes", with a peak of 44.4% in 1954 and 2 seasons at 40% or more.

This explains the numbers: the 10 seasons counted for the name "mercedes" were 2 from the 1950s team plus 8 from the current one, and the 9 of "tyrrell → mercedes" are those same 8 plus Tyrrell's 1972.

What is "the same team"?

"The same factory" is a definition, not an absolute truth. The Tyrrell of 1971 and the Mercedes of 2015 shared a factory, but not owners, people or budgets. Whether they are "the same team" depends on the question we want to answer.

6How much do the conclusions depend on my decisions?

Two decisions shape everything above: how many years to average, and where to draw the line for "dominant". Neither is objective, so I tested both.

The moving-average window

I compared McLaren's line with windows of 1, 3, 5 and 10 years. The more years we average, the flatter the lines become. The biggest successes and the worst failures disappear, and what remains is the long-term trend, which shows the general direction of each team. McLaren's 1988, for example, goes from 93.75% as a single season to 58.3% on 3 years.

Which window is better depends on the question. Shorter windows show individual seasons, but also more noise. Longer windows show the big picture, but hide short or recent eras, such as McLaren's 2025. On the dashboard, the slider lets you try every window from 1 to 10 years.

The dominance threshold

Moving the line from 40% to 30% or 50% changes who is in. The teams in red are the ones that enter or leave:

Seven teams pass all three thresholds: Alfa Romeo, Ferrari, Lotus, McLaren, Mercedes, Red Bull and Williams. For the rest, it depends on where we draw the line. With 50%, Benetton (40.3%), Tyrrell (43.4%) and Cooper (49.7%) drop out.

Any cut-off is arbitrary at the border. Cooper, which peaked just under 50%, would be left out of the chart and the analysis. A threshold is useful to focus on the top teams, but we should always keep in mind that some teams came very close to that level of success and are not shown.

7Methodology, decisions and limitations

Every number in this report depends on these choices. They are all in the notebook, next to the code that applies them.

  • Source: F1DB release v2026.20.0 (data up to the 2026 Brazilian Grand Prix), licensed under CC BY 4.0.
  • Metric: % of races won per season, not points. Points systems and the number of races per season have changed many times, so points are not comparable across eras.
  • Indianapolis 500 excluded (1950-1960). It counted for the World Championship, but it was a separate American race with different cars and teams. Keeping it would understate the dominance of F1 teams (e.g. Alfa Romeo in 1950: 6/6 = 100% instead of 6/7). It was filtered by Grand Prix, not by circuit, to keep the 2000-2007 United States Grands Prix held at Indianapolis. This takes the count from 1,169 races to 1,158.
  • Shared-car wins counted once. In three races (1951, 1956, 1957), two drivers of the same team shared the winning car and both are credited with the win. Each race counts as one win for the team: 1,158 race wins in total.
  • Teams counted by official constructor name, not by factory. For example, Toro Rosso and AlphaTauri are counted separately, Lotus (1958-1994) and Lotus F1 (2012-2015) are different teams, and "mercedes" covers both the 1950s works team and the current team.
  • 3-year centred moving average to show eras instead of year-to-year noise. It smooths peaks (McLaren 1988: 93.75% becomes 58.3%) and is less reliable at the edges (1950 and 2026).
  • Dominant = a peak of at least 40% on the moving average. Selecting the teams with the most total wins was biased towards long-lived teams and hid short eras of dominance, such as Alfa Romeo's. The 40% threshold is a judgement call, and the same one is used for engines and factories.
  • The 2026 season is incomplete (20 races so far), so its values are provisional.
  • Engines counted by their official name. Honda's recent era with Red Bull is split between "honda" and "honda-rbpt" (Red Bull Powertrains, built by Honda), so it looks smaller than it really was.
  • Factories defined by the F1DB constructors chronology, matched by name and year and labelled "first name → last name". "The same factory" is a definition, not an absolute truth: the Tyrrell of 1971 and the Mercedes of 2015 shared a factory, but not owners, people or budgets.

8Data and reproducibility

All the data comes from F1DB, an open database of Formula 1 history, release v2026.20.0, which covers every race up to the 2026 Brazilian Grand Prix. It is published under the CC BY 4.0 licence, which allows reuse with attribution.

The code is on GitHub at AliR-lab/f1-analytics. The whole analysis lives in a single notebook, notebooks/01_dominant_teams.ipynb. To repeat it:

  1. Clone the repository and install the dependencies with uv: 
    git clone https://github.com/AliR-lab/f1-analytics.git
cd f1-analytics
uv sync
  2. Download the CSV files of F1DB release v2026.20.0 and unzip them into data/raw/f1db/. The data/ folder is not in the repository. The notebook uses three files: f1db-races-race-results.csv, f1db-races.csv and f1db-constructors-chronology.csv.
  3. Open JupyterLab and run every cell of the notebook, from top to bottom: 
    uv run jupyter lab
  4. The notebook saves the charts in figures/ and builds the dashboard and this report in docs/.

With the same release you should get exactly the same numbers. A newer release will add races and change the 2026 values, which are still provisional.
